How the result is worked out

The test has five questions for each of the six CEFR levels. The questions start at A1 and get harder. You pass a level when you answer at least three of its five questions correctly.

Your result is the highest level you pass without failing a lower level first. The result page shows your score for every level, so you can see where the questions became too hard.

What this test cannot tell you

This is a short practice test. It checks grammar and reading only. It does not check speaking, listening or writing, and five questions per level is a small sample. Treat the result as a rough guide and not as an official level. For a certificate you need an exam from a recognised exam board.

To learn what each level means, read the guide to CEFR levels. To work on weak points, go to the grammar lessons.

What each CEFR level means

CEFR is the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages, published by the Council of Europe. It describes six levels with statements about what a learner can do. The table below sums up each level in our own words and adds a sentence of the kind a learner at that level can produce. Use it to check whether your test result matches what you can do in real life.

The six CEFR levels in brief
LevelWhat you can usually doTypical sentence
A1Beginner. Introduce yourself, ask and answer simple questions about home and family, and understand slow, clear speech.My name is Ana. I live in Lima with my two brothers.
A2Elementary. Manage short routine exchanges such as shopping and directions, and describe recent events in simple sentences.I went to the market yesterday and bought some fruit.
B1Intermediate. Deal with most travel situations, write a simple connected text on a familiar topic, and explain plans and reasons.I've worked here for two years, and I'd like to learn more about marketing.
B2Upper intermediate. Follow the main ideas of complex texts in your field, talk with fluent speakers without much strain, and give an opinion with reasons.Although the plan is expensive, it would save time in the long run.
C1Advanced. Understand long, demanding texts and implied meaning, and use English flexibly for study and work.Had we known about the delay, we would have booked a later flight.
C2Proficiency. Understand almost everything you read or hear, summarise several sources, and express fine shades of meaning.The proposal, while not without merit, glosses over the cost of maintenance.

Sample questions with explanations

These five questions are written in the same style as the test, one from each of the first five levels. They are not part of the test, so you can use them as a warm-up. Choose an answer for each, then open the key.

  1. A1. She ___ a teacher. (is, are, be)
  2. A2. We ___ to Rome last summer. (go, went, have gone)
  3. B1. If it rains tomorrow, we ___ the picnic. (cancel, will cancel, would cancel)
  4. B2. The work ___ by the time the manager arrived. (finished, had been finished, has finished)
  5. C1. Not until the results came out ___ how close the vote had been. (we realised, did we realise, we did realise)
Show the answers
  1. is. Use is with he, she and it.
  2. went. Last summer is a finished time, so the past simple is correct. Have gone does not go with a finished past time.
  3. will cancel. This is a first conditional about a real future possibility. Use the present after if and will in the result.
  4. had been finished. The work was complete before another past event, and someone else did it. This needs the past perfect passive.
  5. did we realise. When a negative phrase such as not until starts the sentence, the auxiliary comes before the subject.

How to move up one level

Cambridge English estimates that it takes about 200 guided learning hours to move from one CEFR level to the next. The lesson on how long it takes to learn English gives the full figures. Hours alone are not enough, so spend them on the areas that hold you back. Here is a starting plan for each result.

  • A1 or A2. Learn the common irregular verbs, then study a, an and the and in, on and at. Read short graded texts aloud every day.
  • B1. Fix the tense choices that most often block clear writing. Start with present perfect or past simple and conditionals, then write a short paragraph each week about your plans or experiences.
  • B2. Work on the passive, modal verbs and gerunds and infinitives. Read news articles in your field and note new phrases with their context.
  • C1 or C2. Five questions per level cannot separate these levels reliably. Read long texts of different kinds, practise summarising them in your own words, and consider an official exam for a certified result.

Take the test again after a few weeks of study. Your past results stay in this browser, so you can see whether the level where you start to miss questions has moved up.
