Verb forms and limits

The tool uses local English word tags and verb patterns. It does not send your text to a server.

Time and aspect

English courses often teach 12 tense and aspect forms. Simple forms show events or states. Continuous forms show an action in progress. Perfect forms look back from a time.

Will forms can refer to the future. English also uses present forms for future plans. A form name alone cannot tell you the intended time.

Passive and modal forms

A passive form often has be and a past participle, as in was written. The subject receives the action.

A modal such as may, must or should adds a meaning such as possibility, need or advice. The tool names the form. It cannot always tell which modal meaning you intend.

Check clear mistakes

Write He goes, not He go, for an ordinary present statement. After did, use the base form, as in Did she go?

After have or has in a perfect form, use a past participle. Write have gone, not have went.

Use for with a period of time, as in for three years. Use since with a starting point, as in since 2020.

For living somewhere from the past until now, British and American English normally use I have lived or I have been living.

State verbs

For a state, use know or have known rather than am knowing or have been knowing. Other common state verbs include believe, belong, own, understand, want, need, prefer, mean, seem and contain.

The meaning matters. Thinking about a problem, having lunch and seeing a friend are actions. Continuous forms can describe them.

Some continuous forms are fine in special uses. I'm loving it is informal. I've been meaning to call you is also correct.

Meaning still matters

Different tenses can be correct in the same text. This tool does not give a consistency score.

Read, put and some other verbs share present and past spellings. The door is closed can describe a state or an action.

Contractions appear in full in the verb list. The tool may miss even a short sentence when a word gets the wrong tag. Long questions and unusual verbs may need a teacher.

The explanations describe possible uses of each form. They cannot prove the exact meaning of your sentence.
