English idioms dictionary

Search more than 120 common English idioms by word or by theme. Each entry gives a plain meaning, an example sentence and a note on how formal the idiom is.

What is an idiom?

An idiom is a fixed phrase with a meaning that you cannot work out from its separate words. "Kick the bucket" has nothing to do with buckets. It is an informal way to say "die". You have to learn an idiom as one unit, in the same way that you learn a single word.

You usually cannot change the words in an idiom. People say "spill the beans", not "spill the peas" or "drop the beans". Only the grammar changes: "she spilled the beans", "don't spill the beans".

Informal or neutral?

Every entry has a formality label.

  • Informal idioms are fine with friends and in casual messages. Avoid them in essays, reports and job applications.
  • Neutral idioms are safe in most situations, including work emails.

In very formal writing, such as an academic essay, a plain word is usually better than any idiom. Write "very expensive", not "cost an arm and a leg".

How to learn idioms

  • Understand first, use later. You will hear idioms much more often than you need to say them. Learn to recognise many, and choose a few to use.
  • Learn them by theme. Use the theme menu above. Five idioms about money are easier to remember together than five random ones.
  • Learn the whole example. The example sentence shows the grammar around the idiom and a typical situation for it.
  • Check the variety. Some idioms are more common in British English, such as "a storm in a teacup" and "blow your own trumpet". American English says "a tempest in a teapot" and "blow your own horn".
