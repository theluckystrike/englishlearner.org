Main and subordinate clauses

B1 to B2 About 8 minutes

The key idea

A main clause can stand alone. A subordinate clause cannot stand alone and needs a main clause.

Read the rulesTry the quiz

Every English sentence is built from clauses. A clause has a subject and a finite verb. The difference between a main clause and a subordinate clause decides what can stand alone and where the comma goes. This lesson separates the two, then shows the three jobs a subordinate clause can do.

What a clause needs

A clause is a group of words with a subject and a finite verb. The finite verb shows tense. "She waits" is a clause. "Waiting for the bus" is not a clause, because it has no subject and no finite verb. It is a phrase.

Group of wordsSubject?Finite verb?Clause?
the train leavesthe trainleavesYes
after the filmnonoNo, a phrase
because she was tiredshewasYes
to catch the early busnonoNo, a phrase

The main clause

A main clause can stand alone as a complete sentence. It carries the main statement. Every complete sentence has at least one main clause.

  • Main clause: The library closes at six.
  • Main clause: My brother cooked dinner.
  • Main clause: We missed the last train.

One sentence can hold two main clauses. A coordinating conjunction such as and, but, or or so joins them. The result is a compound sentence.

The library closes at six, so we left at five.

Each half of that sentence can stand alone. "The library closes at six" is a sentence. "We left at five" is a sentence. The conjunction joins two equal parts.

The subordinate clause

A subordinate clause has a subject and a finite verb, but it cannot stand alone. It starts with a subordinating word and it depends on a main clause. It adds information: a reason, a time, a condition, a person or a thing.

  • Because the library closes at six, we left at five.
  • We left at five because the library closes at six.

"Because the library closes at six" is not a sentence on its own. A reader waits for the rest. That waiting feeling is the test for a subordinate clause.

Three jobs of a subordinate clause

JobCommon wordsExample
Adverbial: gives time, reason, condition or contrastbecause, although, when, while, if, unless, before, after, so thatWe stayed inside because it was raining.
Relative: describes a nounwho, which, that, whose, whereThe teacher who called me was very patient.
Noun: acts as the object or subject of a verbthat, whether, what, why, howI think that the plan will work.

Word order and commas

The position of the subordinate clause decides the comma.

Correct

Although the room was cold, nobody closed the window.

Subordinate clause first: use a comma between the two clauses.

Correct

Nobody closed the window although the room was cold.

Main clause first: no comma is needed before a short subordinate clause.

Incorrect

Although the room was cold, but nobody closed the window.

Use one connector, not two. "Although" already signals the contrast.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhat happenedFix
When the bell rang.Fragment: a subordinate clause with no main clause.The students left when the bell rang.
I was late, the bus broke down.Comma splice: two main clauses joined by a comma only.I was late because the bus broke down.
She said that she was tired, that was clear.Two statements run together.She said that she was tired. That was clear.
The book who I bought.Wrong relative pronoun for a thing.The book that I bought.

Choosing the right connector

Because gives a reason. Although gives a contrast and does not take but. So that gives a purpose. Unless means "if not", so do not add another negative.

That can introduce a noun clause or a relative clause. Which only describes a thing. In careful writing, which after a comma comments on the whole previous clause, while that limits the noun before it.

  • Defining: The phone that I bought last year stopped working.
  • Commenting: The phone stopped working, which was annoying.
