Idioms in essays and formal writing

B2 to C1 About 8 minutes

The key idea

Use an idiom in an essay only when its register matches the task. Informal idioms usually do not belong in formal writing.

Read the rulesTry the quiz

A learner can know an idiom well and still use it badly. The problem is usually register, not grammar. This lesson explains the three levels of formality, shows where idioms help and where they hurt, and gives four tests to run before an idiom goes into an essay.

The short answer

You can use an idiom in an essay only when the idiom matches the formality of the task and the reader shares its meaning. Most academic essays, reports and exam writing tasks expect neutral or formal English. In those tasks an informal idiom can cost marks even when the grammar is correct.

The question is not "is this idiom good English?" The question is "does this idiom belong in this text?"

Register: formal, neutral and informal

An idiom carries a level of formality, called its register. A dictionary entry for an idiom should tell you the register. Three levels matter for writing.

RegisterWhere it fitsExample
InformalConversation, personal messages, dialogue in a storykick the bucket, hit the sack, grab a bite
NeutralMost writing, including many essays and articlesmake a decision, take part, keep in touch
FormalReports, academic papers, official lettersin accordance with, on behalf of, with regard to

"Kick the bucket" means "die". It is informal and it can sound flippant. In an essay about a person's life, died or passed away is safer. In a comic story, the idiom may be the better choice.

When idioms help

  • Narrative writing. A character who says "I'm over the moon" sounds like a person, not a textbook.
  • Opinion essays with a personal voice. One well-chosen neutral idiom can make a point memorable.
  • Speaking tests. IELTS, TOEFL and Cambridge speaking tasks reward natural language, including some idioms.
  • Journalism and blogs. A headline often uses an idiom because it is short and vivid.

When idioms hurt

  • Academic essays. An idiom can look too casual and can hide a weak argument. "The results were a piece of cake" says less than "The results were easy to reproduce".
  • Exam writing tasks that ask for formal language. The mark scheme rewards accurate register. An informal idiom can lower the score.
  • International readers. An idiom tied to one culture may be unclear to a reader from another country.
  • Technical and legal writing. These texts need exact meaning. An idiom adds ambiguity.

Four tests before you use an idiom

  1. Check the label. Look the idiom up and read its formality label and its example sentence. If the label says informal, do not put it in a formal essay.
  2. Check the reader. Ask whether a reader outside your culture would understand the image. If not, say it plainly.
  3. Check the fit. Read the sentence aloud. An idiom that clashes with the tone of the paragraph pulls the reader out of the argument.
  4. Check the plain version. Write the same idea without the idiom. If the plain version is clearer and shorter, use it.

Safer replacements

Informal idiomMeaningNeutral or formal version
a piece of cakevery easystraightforward, simple to do
cost an arm and a legvery expensiveexpensive, costly
let the cat out of the bagreveal a secretreveal the plan, disclose the information
once in a blue moonvery rarelyrarely, infrequently
get the hang of itlearn how to do somethingbecome familiar with it, learn the method

Common mistakes

MistakeWhy it is a problemFix
Changing the words of a fixed idiomThe phrase stops being an idiom and sounds like an error.Use the wording in the dictionary example.
Mixing two idiomsThe image becomes confused, for example "a piece of the pie in the sky".Choose one idiom and finish it.
Using five idioms in one paragraphThe writing sounds forced and the meaning gets weaker.Keep one idiom and state the rest plainly.
Translating an idiom from your first languageThe result is often not English, even when each word is correct.Check the English dictionary entry before you use it.
